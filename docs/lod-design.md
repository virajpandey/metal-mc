# Far-terrain LOD (Voxy-style, clean-room)

Goal: draw real terrain far past vanilla's render distance on the Metal backend, cheaply enough that a short vanilla render distance plus LOD beats a long vanilla render distance on both frame time and view distance.

Voxy (All Rights Reserved) and its public discussion were studied for techniques only. No Voxy code is used, and Distant Horizons is not used as a reference. `~/research_notes/voxy-techniques.md` has the research notes.

## Data model (v1)

- **Levels.** A level-L voxel covers 2^L × 2^L × 2^L blocks. Level 0 is full resolution: one voxel per block, a quarter region (256 × 256 blocks) per node, built from region files within `lod.detail` blocks of the player (default 768; `METALMC_LOD0` and `-Plod0` override it). Past vanilla's render distance, that ring is where voxels are largest on screen: a 2-block voxel at 200 blocks is about 19 px at 4K.
- **Nodes.** A node is a 256 × 256-voxel column spanning the full world height (y −64 to 320). A level-1 node is exactly one region file (512 × 512 blocks). A level-(L+1) node merges 2 × 2 level-L nodes at half resolution.
- **Voxels.** Each voxel is one byte, a material id from `MetalMCCore.Mat`, the same flat-color material table the standalone engine uses.
- **Downsampling (hybrid).** A 2 × 2 × 2 group is solid if at least 4 of its 8 children are, so terrain rounds to the nearer voxel instead of up. It's also solid if any child is a log or leaves, so single trees survive. Its material is the one most of its 4 columns show from above, so grass, sand and snow survive at a distance.
  - Against the full-resolution reference past 768 blocks, the hybrid scored far error 5.13, err16x 2.99 and holes 0.19%.
  - `ds_top` (any solid) scored 5.33 / 3.49 / 0.16%.
  - `ds_half` (without the tree exception) scored 4.92 / 3.22 / 0.34%: small trees vanish.
  - `METALMC_EXP=ds_top`, `ds_half` and `ds_last` (any solid, last child's material) are kept for comparisons.
- **Deep air is filled.** Before meshing, air and water more than 4 voxels below the lowest open ground within 8 voxels is filled with stone. "Open ground" is the height from which a column is open to the sky, and the lowest one in the neighborhood is taken. This removes cave networks under hills even when they connect to a surface entrance somewhere. They were 49% of all quads, measured on six level-1 regions of the 8 km world (`mmc_debug_lod_mesh_stats`). Entrances stay open to that depth, and arches and overhangs stay open because the ground beside them is open. `METALMC_EXP=nodeepfill` turns it off for comparisons.
- **Unreachable air is filled.** Then air and water that can't be reached from the sky or the node's sides is filled with stone as well (sealed caves).

## Mesh

- **8-byte quads.** `word0 = x | z<<8 | y<<16 | face<<25 | waterDepth<<28` (voxel coordinates within the node, y 9 bits), `word1 = material | (w−1)<<8 | (h−1)<<16 | ao<<24`. That's 8 bytes per quad, against vanilla's 4 × 28 bytes.
- **Greedy merging.** Merges are capped at 16 voxels per side at level 1 and at 64 voxels above that. Only faces with the same material, ambient occlusion and water depth merge.
- **Ambient occlusion, per pixel.** Like vanilla's smooth lighting, each face corner counts the voxels beside it and diagonal to it in the layer in front of the face (0–3). Inside a merged quad every voxel in front of it is open, so only corners on the quad's rim can be occluded. Each quad with an occluded rim stores its rim values, 2 bits each and 2 × (w + h) of them, in a per-node buffer. The fragment shader interpolates the four corners of the voxel face under the pixel. Storing a pattern per quad corner instead kept faces with different patterns from merging: 54% more quads at level 0 and 53% at level 1. At level 0 the shader uses vanilla's steps (1, 0.8, 0.6, 0.4) as they are. Coarser levels use 60% strength, because an LOD corner gradient spans 2 or more blocks where vanilla's spans one. `METALMC_EXP=vertexao` keeps the per-corner version.
- **Per-face colors.** Each material has top, side and bottom colors (grass: tinted top, dirt sides with the tinted fringe, dirt bottom). A full-resolution grass side shows vanilla's fringe: `grass_block_side_overlay`, tinted, over the dirt. Coarser levels keep the average, since the fringe would repeat on every block of a 2-block side.
- **Water is translucent**, like vanilla's. Solid voxels emit faces toward water as well as air, so lake and sea floors are meshed, and water emits faces only toward air. It draws after the opaque quads with vanilla's blending, at the water texture's mean alpha (0.706), with no depth writes (so the occlusion test sees the floor). A face under water stores how many blocks of water are above it (1–15) and is darkened like vanilla's skylight, which loses one level per block of water: the shader uses vanilla's lightmap curve for sky light 15 − depth. Water tops are lowered to vanilla's surface height, 1/9 block below the top of the highest water block. Coarser voxels end on the grid, so at sea level that's 1 1/9 blocks below the voxel top. Floors add about 39% level-1 quads (28% of all quads end up under water, mostly less than 8 blocks deep). `METALMC_EXP=opaquewater` meshes water the old way.
- **Deep water floors are kept.** A shortcut used to skip floors under 12 or more blocks of water from level 2 on. The water above was marked deep (depth code 15) and drawn opaque, blending in a floor color lit at sky light 2. That saved 16% of level-3 quads with no change in the fidelity score on explored terrain. Over generated oceans, though, it drew node-shaped strips far lighter than the real sea. Keeping the floors took the 600-block world's generated-terrain error from 13.48 to 12.99 (14.20 → 12.77 in the high view over the ocean). It costs 9% more quads there and 0.6% on the land-heavy flight, which still held 120 Hz with no dropped frames with TAA on. `METALMC_EXP=skipdeepfloors` brings the shortcut back.
- **Skirts.** The node's sides count as air, so every node emits walls along its edges. These hide cracks where neighboring nodes are at different levels.
  - **Under water they stand in for the floor.** Lit as open air, the solid skirts under the sea showed through the water as bright dashes along node edges wherever the next node's floor lay a few blocks lower. They now carry the depth of the water above them, like any underwater face (under ice too). The water skirts went from the surface down to the floor and put a second layer of water in front of every node edge facing the camera, so the far sea showed the node grid as light and dark bands. Below the top water voxel the skirt is now the column's floor material, lit as that floor. It's opaque, and rays under the surface that cross the node edge see what they'd have found further on: the floor, through one layer of water. Before, at grazing angles, those rays often left the LOD and found sky under the sea. The top water voxel keeps a water skirt only off sea level; at sea level every level's surface lies at the same height.
  - **Tile edges get short skirts too.** Where a node draws some tiles and a finer level draws the others, the levels meet at a tile edge inside the node, where there was no skirt. A coarse voxel's top is the top of the highest block it holds, so the coarse side often lies a block or two higher; the step between them had no face toward the camera, and flying over the land showed thin lines of sky along those edges (found with `METALMC_DUMPQUADS`, which writes the drawn nodes and their quads around a point once the camera reaches it). From level 1 each tile keeps the top two solid voxels of its edge columns (under water, the floor's, lit through the water above them; hung from the surface they missed the steps in lake and river beds, which showed as specks of sky through the water) as side faces toward the neighbor tile, in four extra buckets per tile (one per edge), drawn only when the node doesn't draw that neighbor. That costs under 1% of the quads drawn and no measurable GPU time. `METALMC_EXP=notileskirts` leaves them out.
  - **The reference had the same artifacts.** Scored against nC30, the fix looked worse (edge 13.00 → 13.48), because the old real-data render has the same bands. Against vanilla the near band improved (4.67 → 4.59), and against a reference taken with the fix, the generated terrain scores 12.83 vs 13.27 for the old code.

## Rendering

- **Where it draws.** In Minecraft's main world pass, right after solid terrain (`LevelRendererLodMixin`), encoded natively into the same `MTLRenderCommandEncoder`. It uses the game's projection and reverse-Z depth, so it depth-tests against vanilla. Afterwards Minecraft's pipeline state is re-applied.
- **Tiles.** Each node is meshed as 4 × 4 tiles of 64 voxels, and greedy merges don't cross tile edges along x and z. The quads are stored tile-major, then opaque before water, then by face direction, with each tile's vertical range. The draw culls per tile by view frustum and by face directions that can't face the camera. Adjacent visible face ranges merge into one draw.
- **No section fade-in with the LOD.** 26.3 fades each new chunk section in from the fog color over `chunkSectionFadeInTime` (0.75 s), which hides pop-in at the edge of vanilla's render distance. With the LOD that edge is mid-landscape and the LOD already shows the terrain there, so while flying the fading sections showed as fog-colored ghosts over it. The mod sets the fade time to 0 in the render state while the LOD is active (the saved option is unchanged).
- **Tiles vanilla covers are skipped.** Each frame the mod also lists every section vanilla has compiled in its view area (`SectionOcclusion.recordCompiled`). A level-0 or level-1 tile is skipped when every chunk section it has quads in meets three conditions: the section is compiled, it's within vanilla's horizontal view distance (`ChunkTrackingView.isWithinDistance`), and it's within render distance sections above or below the camera. Vanilla draws those sections if it can see them, and nothing in the others can be seen. Node-edge skirts don't count. The check is per section with LOD geometry (a bitmask per tile), because vanilla doesn't compile sections that are only air.
- **Selection.** A quadtree per frame on the CPU: a node splits into its four children when the camera is within 2 × the child size, and a level-1 node splits into level 0 within the level-0 radius. Missing children are drawn with the parent's quarter tiles. Every node without a parent is a root, not just the top level. Levels are built bottom-up, and the top ones come last (18–25 s for 8 km in-game), so finer levels draw first. Before this, nothing drew until the top level existed. The benchmark now waits for the first full build (`first build done`) before timing.
- **Level transitions fade.** When the quadtree swaps tiles between levels as the camera moves, the new tiles dither in through a 4 × 4 screen-door pattern over 24 frames (0.2 s at 120 Hz). The tiles they replace keep drawing the complementary pattern until the fade ends, even if their node was dropped. Only fading tiles use the discarding variant. There's no fade on the first frame or after a camera jump. Set `METALMC_FADE=<frames>` or `-Pfade`, or 0 to turn it off.
- **Occlusion culling.** Right after the LOD draws, every candidate tile's bounding box is rasterized in the same render pass. The boxes are grown by one voxel, and only their faces toward the camera are drawn, with depth testing on and no depth or color writes. At that point the depth buffer holds vanilla's solid terrain and the LOD. The fragment function has `[[early_fragment_tests]]` and marks the tile visible in a shared buffer. When the command buffer completes, the CPU reads the marks. Tiles whose box was fully hidden in the newest completed test are skipped. Every candidate tile is tested again each frame, so a tile that comes into view reappears 2–3 frames later.
  - Tiles whose box contains the camera are never tested, so they are always drawn.
  - After the camera jumps more than 16 blocks, results tested before the jump are ignored.
  - There's no render pass break and no depth readback, which matters on a tile-based GPU.
  - Translucent terrain, entities and clouds are drawn later in the pass, so they never act as occluders.
  - `METALMC_EXP=noocc` turns it off, and `occnocull` runs the test without skipping anything.
- **Seam, exact per chunk section.** Each frame the mod records the chunk sections vanilla draws, and the LOD gets them as a bitmap (69 × 69 sections around the camera by 24 high). Tiles overlapping vanilla's area use a fragment variant that drops LOD pixels whose voxel lies in one of those sections. Vanilla picks sections by distance in 3D, so this is exact at any camera height. It leaves no gap and never draws LOD over vanilla terrain. Only seam tiles pay for the `discard`, which turns off Apple GPUs' hidden-surface removal. When vanilla draws no sections at all (the camera far above the terrain, or before its chunks load), there's nothing to cut out, and seam tiles use the plain variant. Before that fix, those frames lost every tile near the camera, all water and all fading tiles, because the draw loop stopped at the missing seam pipeline. The earlier seam dropped quads by horizontal distance in the vertex shader. That left a ring between vanilla's round area and the LOD's cut, which showed as holes from above.
- **Same projection as vanilla.** Vanilla draws the level with the camera's projection times view bobbing, the damage tilt and the nausea or portal warp, applied to a copy in `GameRenderer.renderLevel`. The LOD and the section occlusion test take that final matrix (`GameRendererLodMixin`), not the camera's. With the camera's, the LOD jiggled against vanilla's terrain while the player walked: bobbing is up to 0.5° of pitch and 0.3° of roll, about 16 px at the panel's resolution. It also stayed level through the damage tilt (up to 14° of roll). `-PbenchTour=walk` walks with bobbing on.
- **Far plane and fog.** The camera's far plane is pushed to 1.5 × the LOD distance; reverse-Z float depth keeps precision. Vanilla's render-distance fog moves from the chunk edge to the LOD edge. The LOD shader reproduces vanilla's fog formula, so it blends into the same sky.
- **Environmental haze.** The overworld's clear-weather haze (`FOG_END_DISTANCE`) defaults to a linear 0 → 1,024 blocks, which fully hides anything past 1 km; vanilla never draws that far, so it never shows. With LOD active, that default haze is stretched to the LOD distance. Shorter fogs (rain, water, lava, blindness, the Nether's 96 blocks) are unchanged.

## Far terrain from the world generator

Without it, the LOD ends where the world's generated chunks end, at the edge of what the player has explored. In single-player (`lod.generate`, on by default), the mod samples the world generator's own noise for the rest, out to `lod.far`:

- **Requests.** The LOD lists nodes of levels 2 and up (3 in the End) that the quadtree can draw around the player (children of nodes that split, and top-level nodes within `lod.far`). It skips nodes whose regions are all fully generated and returns the rest nearest first (`mmc_lod_far_wanted`).
- **Columns.** `FarTerrain.java` samples 256 × 256 columns per node on 3 low-priority threads, one column per voxel column of the node's level.
  - **Ground height.** The noise router's `chunk_surface_level` is a cheap surface estimate (6 µs), but it runs about 14 blocks low and is off by up to 67. So the search starts 96 blocks above it and marches down the terrain-shape density (`overworld/sloped_cheese`: no caves, aquifers or structures) until it's solid, then bisects. Against the generator's exact height on the 4 km test world, it's 1.3 blocks off on average and within 4 blocks 96% of the time. It costs about 85 µs per column. The exact column scan (`getBaseHeight`) costs 712 µs. These are the samplers vanilla's parallel chunk generation shares.
  - **Biome** at that height (7 µs).
- **From level 2 in the overworld.** Vanilla's noise varies over about 4 blocks horizontally, so 4-block columns (level 2) are the finest that add shape. Level-2 nodes use their own generated columns once they arrive (about 2 s of background generation per node, cached), and a coarser node's interpolated columns until then. Before, levels 0-2 only interpolated level 3's 8-block columns, so unexplored terrain within a few kilometres looked like 8-block blobs, most visibly zoomed in. On the 600-block world, where most of the view is generated, the LOD draws 45% more quads (393 K against 272 K per frame), and a 120 Hz flight dropped no frames. The End stays at level 3: its density is sampled on an 8-block grid. `-PfarMin=3` (`METALMC_FARMIN`) restores the old behaviour.
- **Surfaces** (`LodFar.swift`). Biome names map to surface materials, tints, dense-forest canopy (leaves 5–13 blocks above the ground; sparse woods like cherry groves keep trees on only a share of their tree-sized cells, since a solid canopy there reads as a carpet; dappled forests mix orange, red and yellow poplars on 45% of their cells, with grass between, as real ones look from far off; snowy taigas and groves keep spruces on 55%, with snow between: 12.83 → 12.78 on the edge world), frozen water, and stone on steep slopes in mountain biomes. Below sea level the column fills with the biome's water, and grass and snow give way to the bed under them. Real chunks always win, column by column: generated columns only fill columns with no data.
- **Cache.** Generated nodes are saved per save and seed under `<game dir>/metalmc/lod/far/` (never in the save), about 53 KB per node. Later sessions load them and only generate what's missing.
- **Cost.** 32 km needs about 190 nodes, about 4 minutes of background generation the first time on the 4 km test world. Rendering to 32 km from the high orbit runs at 177 fps with a 6.8 ms p99 frame, against 202 fps for 8 km without generation. Generating during the run didn't change the frame rate.
- **Limits.** Heights come from the terrain-shape density, so caves and structures don't exist, and surface materials are per-biome approximations. Rivers, coasts and mountains land where the real ones will, and the real chunks replace them as the player explores. Multiplayer has no generator on the client.

## Other dimensions

- **One LOD per dimension.** The overworld and the End each get their own `LodWorld` (`mmc_lod_open3`, which returns an id that ingest and far-terrain calls pass back, so a chunk converted after a dimension change can't land in the wrong LOD). The one the player left is paused, not dropped: coming back from the End draws right away. A different save or server drops them all. Region files come from `dimensions/<namespace>/<path>/region` (older saves: `region`, `DIM1/region`), and a server's store is saved per dimension. The Nether has no LOD: its fog ends about 100 blocks out, well inside vanilla's chunks.
- **The End floats.** In the overworld a column of air is a chunk that doesn't exist yet (every generated column has bedrock). In the End it's the void. So End chunks mark their columns with a never-drawn marker material in the bottom voxel, below the End's lowest block. It counts as solid when downsampling, so it reaches every level. The rest of the pipeline then tells explored void from unexplored columns: skirts, far-terrain fill, and a region's "fully explored" test.
  - The deep-cave fill is off: the air under an island is open to the void, not a cave.
  - Downsampling keeps any solid voxel (`.top`), so an island thinner than half a voxel still shows at the coarse levels instead of vanishing.
- **Generated End islands.** `FarTerrain` samples the End generator's final density, which is the islands function, 3D noise and height gradients, at the generator's own cell corners (8 × 4 × 8 blocks). It uses a column-shaped `DensityVolume`, so the islands function is evaluated once per column, not once per sample. Per column it keeps the top and bottom of the island span around the highest solid sample, with the same linear interpolation the generator uses between corners. Void columns cost the same. It's 29 µs per column per thread, a third of the overworld's, and the cache format adds the bottoms (version 2).
- **Light.** 26.x's End has sky light: its `sky_light_color` (#ac60cd) is what tints end stone pink. The LOD samples vanilla's lightmap at sky 15 there, as in the overworld.
- **Undersides.** Air under an island gets sky light only from the side: vanilla's falls off one level per block from the nearest open column. That's why its island undersides are the End's grey-green ambient color, with a pink rim.
  - The mesher reproduces this ("sky cover"). A breadth-first walk from open air through covered air, stopping where the light runs out, gives each covered voxel its light: 15 minus the distance, measured to the voxel's middle at coarse levels.
  - Faces toward covered air carry 15 minus that light in the 4 bits that water depth uses on faces toward water. The shader's existing lightmap lookup then gives vanilla's color.
  - Overworld too: forest floors, the undersides of canopies and overhangs darken as in vanilla. The near-band error against vanilla went 4.79 → 4.65. Light is rounded to steps of 3 so neighboring faces still merge: +5% quads, where exact values cost +12% for 4.59. `METALMC_EXP=noskycover` turns it off outside the End, and `coverexact` uses unrounded values.
- **Test.** `-PbenchTour=end` goes to the End, kills the dragon, and takes the main island in four directions, a high view, and the outer islands 1.5 km out from 110 and 300 blocks up.
- **The dragon.** While the dragon fight's boss bar is up, vanilla fogs the End to 96 blocks, which hides the LOD. After the fight, the islands reach the horizon.

## Ingestion speed and memory

- **Chunk decoding.** `ChunkScan` walks each chunk's NBT once and reads only position, status, block palettes and data, and the surface biome grid; everything else is skipped by length. It inflates with the Compression framework straight into a reusable per-region buffer. It is verified identical to the general NBT decoder (0 mismatches on 3,072 chunks) and takes 0.14–0.21 s per region versus 1.6–2.3 s.
- **Cache memory.** The cached level-2 quadrants are run-length encoded per column: 31 MB instead of 157 MB for 100 regions.
- **Between sessions.** A fully generated region's quadrant is saved under `<game dir>/metalmc/lod/regions/<save>-<dimension>/`, LZFSE-compressed and keyed by the region file's modification time and size and a format version. The next session loads it instead of decoding the region, unless the region has live chunks or is within the level-1 mesh radius, where the full grid is needed anyway. A partly explored region isn't cached: its quadrant includes generated terrain.

## Streaming (v2, `LodWorld.swift`)

- **Change detection.** A background thread polls the region files every 2 s (modification time and size). The integrated server writes chunks when they unload and on autosave, so explored and edited terrain reaches the LOD through the same path as the initial build.
- **Parents without re-reading siblings.** Each region caches its level-2 quadrant (128 × 128 × 96 voxels, 1.5 MB). A parent node is rebuilt from the cached quadrants of its children.
- **The finest level follows the player.** Level-1 nodes are meshed within 1.5 km of the player, re-centered when they move more than 128 blocks, and dropped when they leave that range.
- **Deferred regions.** Regions inside vanilla's render distance (plus 64 blocks) change constantly with autosave. Rebuilding them is deferred until the player has moved away. Before this, 2–4 regions were rebuilt every 2 s; now it's zero while the player stays in the area.
- **Swapping nodes.** Each node owns its Metal buffer, so updates replace nodes atomically while the render thread draws.
- **Live chunks** (`LodLive.swift`, `LiveIngest.java`). Every overworld chunk the client loads is handed to the LOD, and so is every chunk it unloads, which captures edits made while the chunk was loaded. The client thread only copies the chunk's non-empty block sections (`PalettedContainer.copy()`) and samples its 4 × 4 surface biomes at y = 96. A low-priority worker thread maps block states to LOD materials, with a per-state cache and the native classifier, and passes 16 × 16 × 384 material bytes to native code. There they are downsampled exactly like the region reader and stored per chunk at level-1 resolution (8 × 8 columns of 192 voxels, run-length encoded, about 2 KB). When a region is rebuilt, its live chunks replace the region file's version. In single-player this keeps the LOD current without waiting for the server to save.
- **Multiplayer.** A server has no region files, so the LOD is built from live chunks only. The store is saved per server under `<game dir>/metalmc/lod/<address>/overworld/`, one file per region compressed with LZFSE (about 0.8 KB per chunk), and loaded when the player rejoins. The LOD shows the terrain the player has seen on that server, as in Voxy. Config: `lod.live`, `lod.multiplayer`.

## Colors and texture detail

- **Base materials** use the average color of each material's block texture (`LodColors.swift`, from `tools/lod_colors.py`). There are 50 materials: the original 32, plus andesite, diorite, tuff, dripstone, coarse dirt, podzol, mycelium, red sand, red sandstone, six terracotta colors, packed and blue ice, and obsidian. Mountains, badlands, taigas, mushroom fields and frozen peaks each get their own colors.
- **Lighting is vanilla's lightmap.** Each quad samples vanilla's level lightmap once in the vertex shader, as vanilla terrain does: no block light, and sky light 15, or 15 minus depth under water. That covers day, dusk, night, rain darkening, night vision and the darkness effect. Lava samples full block light, so it glows at night. The midnight fidelity tour (`-PfidelityTime=18000`) went from error 10.2 (the old daylight curve left the LOD green and bright with no blue night tint) to 1.6. At dusk (`-PfidelityTime=13000`, references t13000A/B) the error is 1.94, with 0.04% holes and a slight dark bias (RGB −1.3 −1.0 −0.5).
- **Block light.** Torches, lanterns, fire, campfires, end rods and glow lichen are light-source materials: never drawn, but in level-0 nodes a breadth-first walk spreads their light through air and water, one level less per block, as vanilla's does.
  - Glowstone, sea lanterns, shroomlight, jack o'lanterns and froglights are drawn, glow themselves, and light the air around them.
  - Faces carry the light of the air in front of them in word1's top bits, which a retired per-vertex AO experiment used to hold. So lit villages and bases between vanilla's chunks and 768 blocks don't go dark at the seam at night.
  - Past level 0 the sources are kept as a list next to the voxels, since the blocks themselves are too small to keep. Each voxel keeps its brightest source (`LodGrid.lights`, voxel index << 4 | level), downsampling keeps the brightest of each 2 × 2 × 2 group, and the region cache and the multiplayer store save them. At level L a voxel is 2^L blocks, so light drops 2^L per voxel, and a source starts at its level minus half a voxel: the average light over the voxel, not its peak (a torch lights the ground next to it, not a whole 8 × 8 face). Past level 4 nothing is left, so the coarser levels don't carry lights at all.
  - A solid source (lava, glowstone, magma) only counts where its light can get out: a face on air or water. Otherwise buried lava, merged into the stone around it, would light the ground above it, and lava-lake interiors would be most of a world's sources (437K lava blocks in 17 sampled regions, against 656 torches). A source whose voxel became solid lights the air around it a voxel further on.
  - Live chunks carry their sources too, so servers get far light, and a region's live chunks replace the region file's lights along with its blocks (a torch broken since the last save stops lighting).
  - Magma is drawn full-bright like vanilla's (emissive rendering) but gives off light 3, where it used to count as lava (15).
  - On the 4 km test world, levels 1-4 now have 1.8%, 3.6%, 3.8% and 0.7% lit quads. The first build takes about 10% longer (`tools/bench/lodlit.py`; `METALMC_EXP=nofarlight` for the A/B).
  - With sky cover, the midnight tour went 1.62 → 1.28. This also fixed sea lanterns and jack o'lanterns being classified as air (their names contain "lantern") and glowstone as stone.
- **Texture detail** (`lod.textures`, on by default). The LOD samples Minecraft's own block atlas. Each material has a top and a side sprite (`LodColors.swift`, generated by `tools/lod_colors.py`, together with each texture's mean luma). The fragment shader multiplies the flat color by texel luma ÷ mean luma. Colors and biome tints stay calibrated, and a birch log isn't turned brown by the oak texture.
  - The texture repeats once per block, using the world position.
  - The mip level comes from the gradients of the continuous block coordinate, so it doesn't break at tile seams. Far away the smallest mips average back to the flat color.
  - Transparent texels (leaves, ice) darken slightly.
  - Grass sides use dirt, since a 2-block voxel would otherwise show a grass edge on every block of a cliff.
  - It costs about 2% of frame rate (8 km: 316.1 → 310.2 fps).
  - The atlas and sprite table are bound at slots vanilla never uses (texture 30, sampler 15, buffer 20), so vanilla's cached bindings stay valid.
- **Grass, leaves and water are biome-tinted** (`LodBiomes.swift`). The chunk decoder reads each chunk's 4 × 4 surface biome grid (y 96–111, above cave biomes). Tinted voxels use material ids 64 + t, 96 + t and 128 + t for 22 tint classes (vanilla's grass/foliage/water colors), so the tint survives downsampling. `results/lod-v1/far-orbit-biome-tints.png` shows savanna hills matching vanilla's yellow-olive grass across the seam.
  - The colors come from the game. On joining a world and on every resource reload, the mod asks one biome of each class for its grass, foliage and water color (`Biome.getGrassColor` and friends) and hands them to the LOD (`mmc_lod_set_tints`). That follows a pack's color maps and any change in vanilla's biome colors. The built-in table is only the fallback. The first check found 5 of 20 classes off: ocean grass and foliage (8EB971/71A74D, not plains'), snowy plains water (3F76E4), birch by one unit. Snowy taiga and snowy beach keep cold water, so they became their own class.
  - 26.3's dappled forest has autumn colors (grass DF6827, foliage E68E30, water 375154) and poplar trees. Poplar leaves are colored in their textures, not tinted, so yellow, red and orange poplar leaves are materials of their own (ids 160–162, past the tinted ranges). Before this, a dappled forest showed as a green forest. The generator path gives dappled forests a canopy of orange poplar leaves.
  - Huge mushrooms (caps and stems) and ocean monuments' prismarine got materials too (ids 163–167). The caps had been drawn as gray stone in dark forests, and prismarine bricks as red brick. `mmc_debug_unknown_blocks` lists what the classifier doesn't know in a region file. What's left on the test world is underground (26.3's sulfur caves) or rare.
  - `tools/lod_colors.py` checks every row of the generated tables against the `Mat` enum's raw values, since the ids aren't contiguous. An off-by-one there once drew yellow poplars red and orange ones gray.

- **Resource packs.** The base colors come from vanilla's textures, averaged when the mod is built. When the block atlas loads, the mod averages the opaque texels of each LOD material's top and side textures in the loaded pack (`SpriteContents` keeps them in memory). The ratio to vanilla's averages scales that material's colors and texture-detail luma: top faces by the top texture, sides by the side, grass, leaves and water by their base textures. With a test pack (red stone, purple dirt, brighter grass) the far terrain took the pack's colors, with no break at the seam. A pack's biome color maps come through the tints the game reports (above).

## Where the time goes

Per-frame counters from `-PbenchTrace=1` runs: `per_frame_lod_draws`, `per_frame_lod_kquads` and `per_frame_lod_cpu_ms`.

- **The CPU side is cheap.** Selection, culling and encoding take 0.06–0.08 ms per frame for 420–570 draws. GPU-driven selection with indirect command buffers would save almost nothing, so it isn't a priority.
- **The GPU cost is quads.** At 8 km, the LOD submitted 896 K quads per frame and added 0.95 ms of vertex time and 0.55 ms of fragment time to the main pass (1.05 → 2.55 ms). On a tile-based GPU, vertex shading and tiling scale with primitive count, and fragment cost includes per-tile primitive processing. Cutting the quads drawn is what pays.
- **Deep-cave filling** took the 8 km LOD from 896 K to 567 K quads per frame and 267 to 320 fps. It also cut total quads from 11.8 M to 7.7 M and GPU memory from 95 to 62 MB. The screenshots are pixel-identical apart from moving animals.
- **Occlusion culling** matters most at ground level, where hills hide most far terrain. On an 8 km LOD at ground level it cut quads per frame from 579 K to 170 K and draws from 430 to 79. That took the LOD from 337 to 362 fps, against 382 with no LOD at all. From 150 blocks up, only about a quarter of the LOD is hidden. The test costs about as much as it saves there (318–325 fps with it, 320 without). Drawing every box face instead of only camera-facing ones doubled the test's cost and made that view 4% slower.

## Fidelity score

`tools/fidscore.swift` measures how close the LOD comes to real terrain. The fidelity tour (`-PbenchTour=fidelity -Pfidelity=1`: fog off, no clouds, HUD or mobs, noon, clear weather) takes 10 screenshots on the 4 km world. There are 4 at ground level, 4 at y 150 looking down 20°, and 2 at y 260. It runs three times:

- **A:** vanilla at render distance 32, the reference (real terrain out to 512 blocks).
- **B:** vanilla at render distance 12, to find the band the LOD has to fill.
- **C:** render distance 12 plus LOD.

The band is the set of pixels where A and B differ by more than 24 (terrain from about 192 to 512 blocks). Inside it the tool reports:

- **error:** mean color error of C against A after a 4× box downsample.
- **err16x:** the same at 16×, which measures overall color and shading and ignores exact edge positions.
- **holes:** the share of band pixels where C still shows what B shows, meaning no terrain.
- **big:** the share with errors over 48.
- **bias:** the mean signed RGB error.

`FIDSCORE_HEATMAP=<dir>` writes per-view maps: yellow for holes, red where the LOD is brighter, blue where it's darker.

| Run | Change | error | err16x | holes | bias RGB |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| C0 | baseline (average colors, flat shading) | 14.10 | 9.87 | 0.53% | −4.2 +6.3 +2.0 |
| C1 | per-face colors, snow layers | 14.25 | 10.40 | | too bright |
| C3 | ambient occlusion at 60% | 13.47 | 8.82 | 0.55% | −1.0 −0.3 +1.5 |
| C5 | downsampling by top majority | 13.24 | 8.80 | 0.58% | +1.3 +1.1 +4.6 |
| C6 | exact seam | 13.54 | 8.71 | 1.01% | −0.8 −0.0 +1.7 |
| C7 | translucent water, floors under it | 12.15 | 7.39 | 0.30% | +0.3 −1.0 −3.3 |
| C8 | level 0 within 512 blocks | 6.33 | 4.28 | 0.04% | +1.8 −0.1 −1.2 |
| C9 | per-pixel ambient occlusion (35% fewer quads) | 6.35 | 4.30 | 0.04% | +1.9 −0.1 −1.2 |
| C11 | level 0: vanilla's full AO steps, grass side fringe | 5.10 | 3.48 | 0.04% | −0.8 −0.6 −1.5 |
| C13 | transparent texels darkened for leaves, not water | **4.72** | **3.07** | **0.04%** | −1.5 −2.1 −1.9 |

Level 0 halved the error: the band is terrain from 192 to 512 blocks, and 2-block voxels put every terrace edge, tree and shoreline up to a block off. With full-resolution geometry, vanilla's own shading rules (full AO steps, the grass fringe) apply directly. From the mid-height views the LOD is hard to tell from vanilla at render distance 32.

The exact seam fixed the ring of holes from above (high views: 1.47 → 0.54% and 1.10 → 0.33%), but it exposed water. Opaque LOD water had nothing under it and sat about a block above vanilla's surface. At the edge of vanilla's area, rays through vanilla's water came out under the LOD water and found only sky. Translucent water with meshed floors fixed that and matches the reference's visible lake floors: error −10% and holes −70%.

Level 0 costs 0.1 to 0.9 ms, and every view stays well under the 8.33 ms of a 120 Hz frame. Building a level-0 node takes about 1 s (fill and mesh), so the ring is built 6 nodes at a time on the update thread. While a node is missing, its level-1 parent draws that quarter.

Benchmarks bring the game window to the front (`mmc_activate_app`). A window the user can't see is paced to the display's refresh by the window server, and every such run reads 119.9 fps whatever the GPU does.

## Frame pacing

A locked 120 Hz frame is 8.33 ms, so the bench reports frames over 8.33 ms and over 16.7 ms (`frames_over_8ms`, `frames_over_16ms`). It can fly a straight line (`-PbenchFly=<blocks/s>`) so chunks and LOD stream in, and it can log each missed frame with the time (`-PbenchHitches=1`). Native log lines carry timestamps.

- A 60 s flight at 20 blocks/s missed 120 Hz 25 times. The LOD added none of those misses: 20 without it.
- JFR showed G1 young collections every 2–3 s with 5–10 ms pauses, and allocation at about 210 MB/s while chunks streamed in. Part of that was the mod's own section hooks: WrapOperation with `@Local` allocated a holder per captured local and a varargs array per call, per section per layer per frame. `LocalRefImpl` alone was 5% of all allocation.
- The hooks are now allocation-free: a Redirect on `getSectionMesh`, a ModifyArg on `getSectionDraw` that records the layer, and a Redirect on the draw's `List.add`. Dev runs use generational ZGC, with `-Pgc=g1` to compare.
- Together that's 25 → 7 missed frames per minute of flight at the same frame rate. Of the rest, one is the bench's own start screenshot, and some line up with LOD node installs after an update pass.
- **For players:** add `-XX:+UseZGC` to the launcher's JVM arguments.
- **Joining.** The first LOD pass reads every region of the save: 21 s on the 8 km test world with every core. Flying right after joining gave 155 frames over 8.33 ms in the first minute.
  - That pass now runs at background priority, which macOS keeps on the efficiency cores: 14 to 24 frames over 8.33 ms. The LOD appears after about 30 s instead of 11, and the whole pass takes about 63 s: regions 28 s, level 0 17 s, parents 15 s.
  - The update queue itself is background: a queue's priority is a floor that its blocks can raise but not lower. So later passes raise themselves to utility.
  - Regions are processed nearest first, and each level-1 node is installed as soon as it's built. The terrain around the player appears within seconds of joining; the horizon fills in as the rest of the pass completes. Batches (8, 16, 32...) got the first nodes up just as fast but left cores idle at the end of each batch.
  - Later passes, when the player moves, stay at utility priority: 1–3 s each. At background priority they took 9–11 s, too slow to keep level 0 around a flying player. `METALMC_LODQOS=utility` or `background` sets one priority throughout.
  - The LOD's shader library and its seven pipeline variants compile on a background queue the first time the LOD draws, instead of on the render thread (a 21 ms frame when the LOD first appeared).
- **With vsync** (FIFO, the way to play at a locked 120 Hz), the game holds 120.0 fps. A 60 s flight drops 3 frames. Intervals measured on the CPU jitter around 8.33 ms, but the display presents on time.
- **Faster LOD builds.** The mesher scans only each column's face band, from the neighbor's lowest open voxel to the column's top, and the unreachable-air fill marks open sky directly instead of flooding it. The output is identical (quad checksums), and the first full build of the 8 km world went from 43.7 to 20.1 s. Updates as the player moves dropped from 3.5 to 0.9 s.
- **Native panel resolution.** The test Mac's display runs a scaled mode (2056 × 1329 points = 4112 × 2658 pixels) on a 3456 × 2234 panel. Setting Minecraft's fullscreen resolution to 1728 × 1117 at 120 Hz makes exclusive fullscreen switch to the panel's native HiDPI mode (and back on exit). The game then renders 3456 × 2234: 16% more fps (212 → 247 at the mid orbit), and no window-server rescale. In `options.txt` it's `fullscreenResolution:{"width":1728,"height":1117,"red_bits":8,"green_bits":8,"blue_bits":8,"refresh_rate":120.0}` (raw JSON, not quoted). The fidelity references were re-rendered at that resolution (runs `nA`, `nB`, `nR`).

## What didn't pay

- **Mesh shaders** (`METALMC_EXP=meshshader`, `METALMC_MESHQUADS`, `-PmeshQuads`). One thread per quad computes the quad's color, light and AO lookup once instead of in each of its four vertices, then culls quads that face away or lie entirely off one side of the view. The LOD's share of the main pass is 1.69 ms of vertex work and 1.21 ms of fragment work, so vertex work looked like the target. At the mid orbit it ran 198 fps with 64-quad groups, 210 with 32 and 212 with 16, against 212.5 for indexed vertices. The vertex cost is per primitive, not the repeated per-quad work, so only fewer quads help.
- **Per-tile level selection** (`METALMC_EXP=tilesel`, `-PtileSplit`). Picking levels per 64-voxel tile instead of per node draws 21% fewer quads with level 0 to 768 (1.27M → 1.01M, 212 → 236 fps). But whole level-0 nodes had been reaching about 1,100 blocks, and the far band got worse (5.27 → 6.48). With level 0 to 1,024 it matches the node version's cost (1.26M quads) with a slightly better far band (5.18). That's too little to justify the skirts that tile-edge transitions between levels would need against cracks.

- **Temporal anti-aliasing with MetalFX.** MetalFX's temporal scaler at 1:1 worked (the jitter convention was checked by flipping it, which blurred the image: near error 4.87 → 5.00), but at the panel's resolution it cost about 9 ms of GPU per frame: 61.7 fps against 212 in the same flight. MetalFX is built to upscale from a lower resolution. It was replaced by a hand-written resolve (see Known gaps: anti-aliasing).

## Known gaps
- **Edges of explored areas at node borders.** Inside a node, side faces toward a column with no data (an unexplored or ungenerated chunk) are kept only within 8 voxels of the top of the terrain. That leaves a short skirt instead of the terrain's cross-section down to the world bottom. The deep-cave fill ignores such columns, so caves next to them are filled too. On the multiplayer test strip this removed 24% of quads (442,407 → 337,911). Node borders keep full-depth skirts, since they hide cracks between LOD levels where a cliff meets the border. Where explored terrain ends exactly at a node border, the cross-section still shows.
- **Edits while a chunk stays loaded** reach the LOD when the chunk unloads (or when the save changes, in single-player), not immediately. They're mostly inside vanilla's range anyway.
- **Screen-size selection.** Levels switch by distance: a node splits when the camera is closer than its size. Zoomed in (the spyglass, or a field of view under about 56°), distances are divided by the zoom relative to vanilla's 70° (`METALMC_EXP=nozoomlod` turns that off). At field of view 30 that drew 33 level-1 nodes instead of 21. Finer levels only exist near the player, so past about 1.5 km the zoomed view still uses level 2. Past the level-0 ring that gives voxels of about 4–8 px at 4K. Voxy switches by projected size, 64 px per 32-voxel section. `-PlodSplit` (`METALMC_SPLIT`) sets the split factor (default 2): at 3 a node keeps its finer children out to 3× their size, so voxels past the level-0 ring are at most about 4 px at the panel's resolution instead of 6. 3 is the default: on the flight bench it draws 18% more quads (1.89 M vs 1.58 M per frame) for about 1 ms more GPU uncapped (206.7 → 186.2 fps), and at 120 Hz with vsync the flight dropped 2 frames against 1 at split 2 (with TAA: 1 against 0). The fidelity score only covers terrain out to 512 blocks, since vanilla can't render farther to compare against.
- **Level 0 from live chunks.** Live chunks within 1,280 blocks of the player (the level-0 radius + 512) are also kept at full resolution: tinted, LZFSE-compressed, in memory only. Level-0 nodes are built from the region file (single-player), then these on top, so servers get a level-0 ring too. On the multiplayer test strip that drew 10 level-0 nodes (408K quads) where it had none. Chunks beyond that radius are dropped, so after a long trip the ring comes back only as the server resends chunks. In single-player, a level-0 node is built when it comes into range, from whatever the region file holds then. Chunks the server hasn't saved yet are filled with generated terrain. Near the player, changed regions wait until the player leaves, so newly explored level-0 terrain can stay approximate for a while. That only shows where vanilla doesn't draw, such as far above the terrain.
- **Past about 32 km, cube voxels stop working.** A level-8 voxel is 256 blocks tall and a level-9 one 512, more than the world's 384-block height, so mountains 65 km out would flatten to one-voxel slabs. Raising the level cap isn't the way to the horizon. The plan is a height-field band past the LOD: seed-sampled heights and colors on coarse rings, ray-marched per pixel, which keeps full vertical precision at any distance.
- **The far-band reference nR2 predates the 26.3 materials** (poplars, the dappled forest's tints), so it shows the test world's autumn forest green. Score far terrain against a current real-data render instead (nC32 for RD 12 + LOD 8192, taken with the underwater skirts; nC30 before them): generated terrain on the 600-block world scores 12.83 against nC32 (12.99 against nC30; 14.27 before level-2 generation, 13.48 before deep floors were kept).
- **Block light past level 4** (about 4 km) is gone: a source's light spreads less than one 16-block voxel. Only self-lit blocks (glowstone, sea lanterns, lava, magma) glow there.
- **Anti-aliasing is on by default** (`taa=false` in config/metalmc.properties turns it off; bench runs keep it off unless `-Ptaa=true`). Without it, neither vanilla nor the LOD is anti-aliased: at the native panel resolution a block 700 blocks away is about 2.4 px, so distant edges crawl a little in motion. With it, the level (the LOD too) is drawn with an 8-step Halton sub-pixel jitter, and before the hand and HUD one compute pass (`Taa.swift`) blends the frame into an accumulated history:
  - The history is reprojected per pixel from depth (the nearest of each 3 × 3 neighborhood, so edges move with the nearer surface) and the camera's motion. It's sampled with a 5-tap Catmull-Rom filter, which stays sharp in motion.
  - It's then clipped to the current neighborhood's color range in YCoCg (mean ± 1.25 standard deviations), so newly revealed surfaces and moving mobs don't smear, and mixed 1:9 with the current frame (up to 3:7 when moving fast).
  - A draw copies the result back into the frame with mild contrast-adaptive sharpening. Averaging jittered frames is a box filter over each pixel, which softens texture detail. `METALMC_TAABLEND` and `METALMC_TAASHARPEN` tune the blend and the sharpening.
  - The history is RGB10A2: an 8-bit history stalls up to 5 levels short of its target at a 10% blend, and a 16-bit float one cost 0.3 ms more. Each 16 × 16 threadgroup loads its pixels and a 1-pixel border into threadgroup memory once.
  - At 3456 × 2234 the resolve takes 0.84 ms and the copy 0.31 ms (a standalone benchmark on the M3 Pro). The first version, sampling the textures directly with a 16-bit history and a blit, took 1.62 ms.
  - Flying at 20 blocks/s, walking with view bobbing past mobs, zooming to a 30° field of view, rain, campfire smoke with animals around it, third person and a fast turn (100°/s) showed no smearing or ghosting (`-PbenchTour=taa`, `tools/bench/taa_tour.sh`). The hand and HUD are drawn after it.
  - In game at 3456 × 2234 it costs about 1.8 ms of GPU per frame (uncapped flight 199 → 169 fps). At 120 Hz with vsync the flight dropped no frames with it, and 1 without.
